ABOUT

Not a game about going fast.
A game about getting there.

The stage is beyond the sea and beyond the mountains, where no road can take you. The 1909 Channel crossing, the 1919 Atlantic crossings, the flights over the Alps and the Andes — you fly fictional courses inspired by these real challenges, in a biplane with a wooden propeller. Prepare the plane, read the sky, watch the gauges, and sometimes turn back. That is how you reach a faraway goal.

Prepare in the hangar

The propeller type (climb or cruise), how much fuel to load, a weather radio or a spare propeller. And you tighten the bolts with your own hands. What you choose decides which mountains you can clear and which seas you can cross.

Fly by the gauges

Throttle and mixture, altitude and carburetor heat. Reading ground speed, the tachometer, exhaust temperature and the fuel gauge, you stretch your "Range" a little further.

The courage to turn back

A thunderstorm ahead. Fuel down to the reserve. Over the sea, if the engine stops there is no field to land in. Decide before the "point of no return". A brave retreat is properly rewarded in your score.

FEATURES

Highlights

01

Crossing the sea — a sky with nowhere to land

Over the sea, an engine failure means ditching. Even with a spare propeller you can't swap it. The strip at the bottom of the screen shows, as a red line, the farthest point from which your speed and fuel can still get you back — the point of no return.

  • Flight notes: ground speed, range, ETA and weather ahead
  • A top-down map and a side profile of your altitude
  • Gen, your mechanic, chimes in with advice as you go

02

Over the mountains — thin air and freezing heights

The higher you go, the slower you climb — at 3000 m, half as fast. Leave the valley heavy and you may not clear the pass. High up the headwind is strong, and above the freezing level ice builds on the wings and propeller. At high fields, takeoff needs a longer run too.

  • A climb propeller is strong in the mountains; a cruise propeller is strong over the sea
  • Snow-capped peaks, bare rock, and cloud clinging to the summits

03

Hangar and stops — what you do before flying matters most

Choose your destination on the map, check the profile and forecast for each leg, then decide on the propeller, fuel and equipment. At each stop, inspect the plane, repair, refuel and stay the night. If you're unsure, the "mechanic's recommended setup" tells you what to take and why.

  • Rumors from villagers, fishermen and mail pilots hint at the sky ahead
  • Tightening the bolts is a timing mini-game

04

New to it? A tutorial that pauses to teach

In the two tutorials, the Sea Route and the Mountain Route, the flight pauses at every important step. The gauge to watch lights up, and the card explains what it means and what to do. Complete the "Try it" task and you get a "Done!".

  • Climbing, mixture, cruising and reading your fuel, one at a time
  • Explanations for first encounters too: cloud, thunderstorms, mountains, the sea, icing

WEATHER & PILOT

The sky shows a different face every time

The weather doesn't always follow the forecast. Carry a weather radio and you can get a fresh forecast in flight. And fatigue and haste dull the pilot's judgment.

Difficulty

Easy
Mixture and carb heat are automatic. You handle power, altitude and route.
Normal
Only the bolt-check notice is automatic. You do almost everything yourself.
Real
Nothing automatic. The gauges show numbers only, with no color guides. Forecasts miss by more.
